# Live character voices are buildable, and still unclaimed

Every piece needed to give each character its own voice during live read-aloud now exists, and no major reading app has put them together. Deciding who speaks a quote is close to solved for tagged dialogue (95 to 99% correct) and still wrong on roughly one untagged line in ten in the best published system, so the design has to make a wrong guess cheap: fall back to the narrator voice when unsure. Inworld's current model, `inworld-tts-2`, accepts a script of up to 100 lines with a different stored voice per line in one request and carries audio history between the lines, which is the exact request shape this feature needs. Language-model cost is small, about 4 to 50 US cents per novel by estimate, against roughly $12 to $15 of speech for a novel heard in full, so latency and accuracy should drive the design, not model price. Automatic per-character voices are not a new idea: ElevenLabs, Google, Spoken and Inworld's own Audio Studio do it for authors as an offline render, and three small apps (BookNPC, Audora, Tambr) claim it for readers. None of them documents pressing play on any page and hearing the cast at once, and none is a web reader, so a live in-reader version would be a first of its kind as far as the research found. Build it as a layer over today's single-voice playback that starts in the narrator voice with no added wait and adds character voices as soon as attribution for the next passage is ready.

## Tagged quotes are solved; untagged exchanges still miss one in ten

The benchmark that matters is PDNC, 22 to 28 full English novels with about 36,000 annotated quotes, of which roughly a third carry a named tag ("said Elizabeth"), 29% a pronoun or description ("he said"), and 37% no tag at all ([PDNC paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.05836)). The best published result is **94.5% overall**, from an encoder model released in August 2026 as ModernBookNLP ([Michel, Attali, Epure 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02359v1)). The best published language-model result is about 90%, from Llama-3 8B used zero-shot ([Michel et al., NAACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11380v3)). The old default, BookNLP, attributes only 40 to 63% of PDNC quotes correctly off the shelf and is effectively unmaintained ([Vishnubhotla et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03734v1); [BookNLP repository](https://github.com/booknlp/booknlp)).

| System | Overall | Named tag | Pronoun tag | No tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BookNLP off the shelf (2022) | 63% | 94% | 46% | 46% |
| Llama-3 8B, zero-shot (2024-26) | 89.8% | 94.9% | 93.1% | 81.8% |
| ModernBookNLP joint scoring (2026) | 94.5% | 99.3% | 95.5% | 89.3% |

Sources: [PDNC paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.05836); [Michel, Attali, Epure 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02359v1).

These numbers are upper bounds for a real app. They assume the quotes are already found, a correct character list with aliases is supplied, and the books are classic novels with standard quotation marks. The closest thing to a full-pipeline figure, with no character list given, is a score of 77.5 on LitBank excerpts ([Michel, Attali, Epure 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02359v1)). **No one has published accuracy for any current hosted model** (Gemini, GPT, Claude, Qwen) on this task. The only hint is that Llama-3 70B made 3 errors in 1,442 quotes on one unseen 2024 novel, which the authors call easier than average ([Michel et al., NAACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11380v3)). A small labelled test on the app's own chapters is therefore the only way to know what the chosen model does.

The language-model recipe with evidence behind it is specific. Find and number the quotes in code, send a chunk of about 4,000 tokens containing many quotes, include the closed character list with aliases, ask for brief reasoning, and take back a map of quote number to canonical name. Feeding the previous chunk's answers into the next lifted accuracy by about a point on untagged quotes, and without the character list the model invented names outside it ([Michel et al., NAACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11380v3)). Asking about one quote at a time, as a 2023 study did with gpt-3.5, reached only 71% ([Su et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.14590)). Scoring all quotes in a shared window together beats scoring each alone, 94.5% against 92.6% in the encoder work ([Michel, Attali, Epure 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02359v1)). Context size matters for a concrete reason: with a 200-token window, 14% of quotes have no mention of their true speaker in view, and at 2,000 tokens that falls to about 2% ([Michel et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11368v2); [Michel, Attali, Epure 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.02359v1)). A plain rule for named tags is still worth keeping, because the language model was worse than a trained model on exactly those easy quotes, 94.7% against 98.6% ([Michel et al., NAACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11380v3)).

What stays hard is well mapped. Long untagged back-and-forth is the largest error source for every system, and simple speaker alternation is right only 52% of the time when used alone ([PDNC paper](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2204.05836)). Characters who share a surname get merged, and whole-book coreference tops out near 67 where short documents reach about 80, which is why every working system links quotes to a character list instead of resolving every pronoun ([Vishnubhotla et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03734v1); [BookCoref](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.12075v1)). Nested quotes, letters, diary entries, quoted thoughts and free indirect speech are excluded from the benchmark, so no error rate exists for them ([Vishnubhotla et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.03734v1)). Books without quotation marks, or with dash-marked dialogue, have no published language-model benchmark at all; a commercial vendor lists missing quotation marks as its first known failure ([AudioProducer.ai](https://dev.to/audioproducer-ai/how-audioproducerais-auto-assign-pipeline-turns-a-chapter-into-a-multi-voice-audio-drama-3daa)).

## Studios cast characters offline; no reader does it on demand

The market splits in two. Author-side studios detect characters automatically and render ahead of time with a human check. Listening apps play at once in a single voice. Nothing from a major vendor does both.

ElevenLabs launched Character Casting in its Audiobooks studio on 30 July 2026: it detects the characters, proposes a voice for each, and applies a voice change to every line that character speaks ([ElevenLabs blog](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/introducing-character-casting-in-audiobooks)). Its own documentation says detection "should be treated as a starting point", may miss minor or unnamed characters, allows up to 150 detected voices per book, and charges credits to regenerate after a voice change ([ElevenLabs Audiobooks docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-creative/products/audiobooks/llms.txt)). Google Play Books has a "Detect characters" button for publishers, English only, with manual line assignment ([Google Play Books Partner help](https://support.google.com/books/partner/answer/14183080?hl=en)). Inworld's Portal product, Audio Studio, does the same on TTS-2: quotes go to the name beside them, unplaced lines go to an "Unnamed speaker", an optional AI pass sets the speaker of each paragraph, and a voice is picked from the library using each character's lines and description ([Inworld Audio Studio](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/audio-studio.md)). On the consumer side, the research found no primary source showing per-character voices for imported books in ElevenReader, the Speechify reader, Kindle's Assistive Reader or Apple Books; Kindle uses the device's own speech engine ([ElevenLabs help](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/help-center/product/distribution-publishing/eleven-reader/what-is-eleven-reader); [Amazon help](https://digprjsurvey.amazon.com/csad/help/node/TdHCSXXqsdhaVKt3Tc)).

Open source tells the same story. The popular converters are single-narrator; ebook2audiobook has 20,700 stars and no per-speaker mode surfaced ([ebook2audiobook](https://github.com/DrewThomasson/ebook2audiobook)). The multi-voice projects have moved from BookNLP to language-model annotation. Alexandria has a model emit one entry per line with speaker, text and delivery direction, passes the character roster and the last three entries between chunks, and ships a second pass to strip "said he" out of character lines ([Alexandria](https://github.com/Finrandojin/alexandria-audiobook)). audiobook-creator builds a character list with age and gender first, then attributes each line against it ([audiobook-creator](https://github.com/prakharsr/audiobook-creator)). Castwright keeps a character's voice across a series and checks output with speech recognition and a speaker-fingerprint test ([Castwright](https://github.com/dudarenok-maker/Castwright)). All are offline batch jobs that need a local GPU or a model endpoint, and none publishes a measured error rate.

Three small apps come closest to the proposed feature. BookNPC says its AI "works out who is speaking and gives each character its own voice", picks voices by gender and age, and lets the user swap them, but its studio audio is "rendered ahead of time on cloud GPUs" ([BookNPC site](https://booknpc-website.pages.dev)). Audora launched through Y Combinator on 10 November 2026; it lets the reader begin listening while the rest is generated, and the coverage notes it has not been tested on full books ([Fondo blog](https://fondo.com/blog/audora-audiobook-character-voices)). Tambr, on iPhone, does the same for pasted text and fan fiction, and shipped with no way to change a voice, which a user complained about ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6759809866); [Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/p/tambr/tambr)). None of these claims could be tested, none discloses its method or its time to first audio, and none is a web reader.

So the answer on novelty has two parts. The concept is old; patents on assigning voices per character date from 2012 to 2015 ([US 8,326,629](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8326629)). The research found no product, tool or paper that documents speaker-aware narration generated just ahead of a listener from any point in a book, so a live web version would be new. The gap is fresh and contested: Audora is funded and progressive, and ElevenLabs owns detection, dialogue models and a consumer reader, which makes it the most likely large player to close it.

## Cast from the catalog by description, then freeze the voice ID

The published recipe closest to a fixed catalog is AuDirector's two steps: embed a text description of every catalog voice and of each character profile, keep the nearest candidates, then let the language model choose among them with the character's actual lines in view. It scored 4.00 on human-rated voice-role matching against 3.48 and 3.09 for two earlier systems, and its authors blame the weaker results on coarse metadata and on asking the model to choose from the whole library at once ([AuDirector](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.11866)). The evidence is thin, with small tests and mostly machine judges, and in the one comparison with a commercial product the product won on human ratings ([MultiActor-Audiobook](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13082v1)).

Which character attributes to trust is measured. On classic novels small models extract **gender at 0.99**, a four-band age at about 0.80 with errors mostly between neighbouring bands, and **origin at only 0.42**; the authors warn the books were probably memorised, so these are upper bounds ([S-VoCAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00958)). Accent should therefore not be cast from a guess. Speech systems also drift toward US and UK accents even when asked for others, and personality-only voice prompts produce male-sounding voices by default ([BAAP 2026 abstract](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/al/research/baap2026/programme/kind-is-the-opposite-of-competent-phonetic-variation-in-english-tts-voices.pdf)). Gender needs an "unknown" value that falls back to the narrator or a neutral voice. Profiles built from the whole book know the ending, and timeline-unaware character systems are a named source of spoilers, so anything shown in a cast list must be limited to what the reader has reached ([Yuan et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12726v2); [arXiv 2512.07474](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.07474)).

Inworld's catalog suits this approach. The list endpoint returns for each voice an ID, a description, tags, and structured `gender` and `ageGroup` fields, and can be filtered on them; there is no accent field, so accent lives in the locale and the description ([Inworld list voices](https://docs.inworld.ai/api-reference/voiceAPI/voiceservice/list-voices.md)). The public voices page held 409 system voices on 25 November 2026, 202 of them English, including audiobook narrators, villains, children and regional voices ([Inworld system voices](https://inworld.ai/voices)).

Consistency comes from one simple device in every system studied: a stored map of character to voice ID, read on every synthesis call. The speech model does not remember. Designed voices follow the same rule. Inworld states that "repeating the same description does not guarantee the same voice across requests", so a designed voice must be published and reused by its ID, and saved voices count against a plan limit ([Inworld voice design](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/voice-design.md)).

No research studies a cast larger than the voice pool, so the tiering below rests on tool practice and narrator craft. Give the narrator one fixed voice. Give the characters with the most lines a unique voice each. Let minor characters share a small pool, and only with characters they never speak alongside. Send unresolved lines to the narrator, as Google Play Books does with unassigned text ([Google Play Books Partner help](https://support.google.com/books/partner/answer/14183080?hl=en)). Two catalog voices with the same labels can sound nearly alike; randomly drawn synthetic voices are far closer to each other than real speakers are ([Synth2Aug](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2011.11818)). Every shipping tool lets the user change any casting and applies the change book-wide ([ElevenLabs blog](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/introducing-character-casting-in-audiobooks)).

## Inworld's script request fits narrator-plus-cast in one call

The model line-up changed during 2026. The current models are `inworld-tts-2` and `inworld-tts-2-flash`; `inworld-tts-1` and `inworld-tts-1-max` were discontinued on 15 June 2026 and the 1.5 models are deprecated ([Inworld TTS models](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/tts-models.md)). Only `inworld-tts-2` supports steering, automatic direction and script synthesis, so it is the model for this feature. Code written for the 1.x API needs two checks: `temperature` is ignored on TTS-2 in favour of `deliveryMode`, and since 6 August 2026 a steering tag stays in force until another tag or `[reset]` ([Inworld API reference](https://docs.inworld.ai/api-reference/ttsAPI/texttospeech/synthesize-speech.md); [Inworld release notes](https://docs.inworld.ai/release-notes/tts.md)).

**Script synthesis** is the key capability. A request carries `script.utterances[]`, each with its own text and stored voice ID, and returns either one combined recording or a separate clip per utterance. Inworld says the model "keeps a bounded audio history within the script to help later utterances follow earlier turns naturally. Separate requests do not share this history" ([Inworld script synthesis](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/capabilities/script-synthesis.md)). Per-utterance output comes with word timestamps measured from the start of each clip, which suits per-line caching and word highlighting. Scripts work on the unary, HTTP streaming, async and batch endpoints, and not on WebSocket or the OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Every voice in a script must be a stored voice.

| Item | Value on `inworld-tts-2` |
|---|---|
| Utterances per script | 1 to 100 |
| Text per request (sum of utterances) | 2,000 characters unary; 4,000 HTTP streaming; 100,000 async job (10,000 on the free On-Demand plan) |
| Per-utterance controls | voice ID, speaking rate 0.5 to 1.5, delivery mode |
| Time to first audio (P90, server side) | 100 ms; 20 ms for Flash |
| Concurrent generations | 5 On-Demand, 10 Creator, 50 Builder |
| Price per 1M characters | $25 On-Demand, $20 Creator, $17.50 Builder; batch 20% less |
| Custom voices (designed plus cloned) | 100 On-Demand, 500 Creator, 3,000 Builder |
| Voice design calls | 2 per minute On-Demand, 3 Creator |

Sources: [script synthesis](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/capabilities/script-synthesis.md); [TTS models](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/tts-models.md); [concurrency limits](https://docs.inworld.ai/resources/concurrency-limits.md); [job limits](https://docs.inworld.ai/resources/job-concurrency-limits.md); [billing](https://docs.inworld.ai/portal/billing.md); [pricing](https://inworld.ai/pricing). Plan prices and voice limits were read through a page summariser and should be rechecked on the live pricing page.

Steering is free-form on TTS-2. Text in square brackets is either a recognised sound such as `[laugh]` or `[sigh]`, or a natural-language instruction such as `[whisper in a hushed style]`; pauses use `<break time="1s" />`, limited to 20 per request. Instructions are billed as characters, and Inworld advises against re-tagging every sentence because it makes delivery uneven ([Inworld steering](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/capabilities/steering.md); [pause controls](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/capabilities/pause-controls.md)). One consequence for books: literal square brackets in the text will be treated as instructions, so they must be stripped or rewritten before synthesis. An optional Reasoning mode has a language model write the delivery instruction, guided by a context string of up to 600 characters such as "Narrator of a psychological thriller audiobook." ([Inworld steering](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/capabilities/steering.md)). There is no pitch parameter; pitch is steered in text.

Voice design takes a description of up to 1,000 characters plus a preview script, returns up to three previews, and a publish call turns one into a permanent voice ID ([Inworld design endpoint](https://docs.inworld.ai/api-reference/voiceAPI/voiceservice/design-voice.md)). The preview script shapes the voice, so a character's own dialogue is the natural script. On the free plan the 100-voice limit and two design calls per minute make a designed voice per character per book impractical, and Inworld notes that child voices clone poorly ([Inworld voice cloning best practices](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/best-practices/voice-cloning.md)).

Several things are undocumented and need a test before the design relies on them: whether steering tags work inside a script utterance and persist into the next one, whether Reasoning combines with scripts, how long the "bounded audio history" is, and how much silence is inserted between utterances in combined output. Among hosted alternatives only ElevenLabs accepts an arbitrary list of speaker-tagged lines, at $80 per million characters against Inworld's $25; Gemini's multi-speaker speech is limited to two prebuilt voices ([ElevenLabs API pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api); [Gemini speech generation docs](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/speech-generation)).

## Start in the narrator's voice and let the cast catch up

Every tool and paper the research found annotates a whole book or chapter before rendering, so a just-ahead design is new work with no published timings. The pieces transfer directly, and the hybrid that follows from them is the recommended shape: begin playback at once in the narrator voice, run a character-list pass over the book in the background, attribute one chapter at a time, and switch to character voices from the next unsynthesised passage once its attribution is ready. If anything fails or runs late, the listener hears today's single-voice behaviour and waits for nothing.

Model output should be references, never text. Models asked to re-emit text alter it; one study saw "'em" rewritten as "them", and the tools that have the model re-emit the script need a clean-up pass for narration stuck in character lines ([LLMLink](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.751.pdf); [Alexandria](https://github.com/Finrandojin/alexandria-audiobook)). The robust shape is for code to find and number the quoted spans, for the model to return only quote number, speaker ID from the supplied list and a confidence label, and for code to check that every quote appears once and every speaker is on the list, with one repair call on failure and the narrator voice for whatever stays unresolved. Thinking modes are reported to break strict JSON and add delay, so they should be off, with the provider's structured-output mode on ([Alexandria](https://github.com/Finrandojin/alexandria-audiobook)). One builder's fix for a tag spoken in the character's voice was the same principle: split on quote position in code ([scriptorium PR #15](https://github.com/theraccoonbear/scriptorium/pull/15)).

Cost is not the constraint. Using official prices read on 25 November 2026, the research estimated a 100,000-word novel at about 333,000 input and 50,000 output tokens for a character-list pass plus numbered attribution, which is roughly **$0.05 on Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite and $0.23 on Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite**; re-emitting text doubles to quadruples that ([Gemini API pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). These are arithmetic estimates, not measurements, and thinking tokens billed as output would multiply them. Speech dominates: a novel is about 600,000 characters, which is about $15 at Inworld's On-Demand rate, or an estimated $1.25 to $1.50 per listening hour ([Inworld pricing](https://inworld.ai/pricing)). Character voices do not add characters to synthesise, apart from steering tags. A live reader that synthesises only what is played also avoids the per-render cost that every studio product charges for books a reader samples and drops.

For speech, a script request over HTTP streaming holds 4,000 characters, a few minutes of audio, with per-utterance clips arriving in order. The stream is newline-delimited JSON with base64 audio, so the edge function must decode line by line and cannot pass it through as an audio body ([Inworld streaming](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/synthesize-speech-streaming.md)). Audio history does not cross requests, so cuts between script requests belong at paragraph or scene breaks, with the last few lines passed as `synthesisContext.previousRequests`, which is unbilled ([Inworld best practices](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/best-practices/generating-speech.md)). Separately generated short clips reset their pitch and end with a falling tone, which is the reason to prefer script requests over one call per quote ([switchboard issue #94](https://github.com/Kayne19/switchboard/issues/94)). With five concurrent generations on the free plan, a small worker pool with backoff on HTTP 429 is enough ([Inworld concurrency limits](https://docs.inworld.ai/resources/concurrency-limits.md)).

In the browser, the documented pattern for joining clips is to decode each into a buffer and schedule it on the audio clock at a running start time, with a small buffer queued ahead to absorb network variance ([Palabra docs](https://docs.palabra.ai/docs/streaming_api/realtime_tts); [Luke Oliff](https://lukeocodes.dev/streaming-tts-rethinking-audio-pipeline)). MP3 clips carry encoder padding at both ends, harmless at sentence breaks and audible when a sentence is split mid-clause, so WAV or PCM is safer for clips that join inside a sentence ([Google WebFundamentals](https://github.com/google/WebFundamentals/pull/4121/files)). The research found no source on loudness differences between voices or on lock-screen playback with this approach on phones; both need testing on a real device. Caching each clip under a key of text, voice ID, model and settings means a voice change invalidates only that character's lines.

## Listeners want the cast in dialogue and the narrator everywhere else

No peer-reviewed study compares single-voice and multi-voice audiobooks on comprehension or fatigue. The one measured comparison is a vendor-commissioned survey of about 1,000 US fiction listeners: an AI multi-cast excerpt beat a human single narrator on favourability (61% to 53%) and engagement (58% to 49%) in character-driven scenes, the human won on plain exposition, and 81% of frequent listeners said they were interested in distinct character voices ([Podnews press release](https://podnews.net/press-release/edison-spoken)). It was paid for by a multi-cast vendor, used one title, and showed no significance tests ([Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/pw/by-topic/digital/content-and-e-books/article/100825-some-favor-ai-narration-for-multi-cast-audiobooks-even-as-interest-in-ai-voices-wanes.html)). Against it, willingness to try AI narration fell from 70% to 61% in the Audio Publishers Association's 2026 survey ([PPC Land](https://ppc.land/audiobook-sales-hit-2-43-billion-in-2025-as-157-million-americans-tune-in/)). Laboratory work shows a change of talker carries an attention cost even when the switch is predictable, and that the cost does not grow with the amount of variability, which suggests switches per minute matter more than cast size ([Frontiers in Neuroscience](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4403343/); [Cognition 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010027720302122)).

Craft convention is consistent and restrained. The narrator reads narration and dialogue tags, and the character voice reads only the words inside the quotation marks. Tags stay in the audio because they carry action and emotion ([Vocal Copycat](https://www.vocalcopycat.com/articles/dialogue-attribution-audiobooks)). Narrators differentiate by attitude and tempo more than pitch: "voices will soon start sounding alike if you only make changes to pitch", and "It's better not to do the accent than to do it poorly" ([ACX blog](https://www.acx.com/mp/blog/how-to-act-like-an-audiobook-narrator)). Audible names four styles (unvoiced, partially voiced, fully voiced, full cast) and warns that "too many distinct character voices can be a little distracting" ([Audible UK blog](https://audible.co.uk/blog/which-audiobook-narrator-will-i-like)). In a first-person book the narrator is a character, and duet convention gives that character's narration and dialogue to the same performer ([Karen Commins](https://karencommins.com/2015/02/dual-gender-narrations-in-audiobooks.html)). No authoritative convention was found for letters, quoted documents or italic thoughts.

No study measures what a wrong voice costs the listener, or what error rate people forgive. The reasoning for narrator fallback is that a wrong voice asserts something false about who is speaking, while the narrator voice asserts nothing and is the long-standing norm. Builders describe attribution as "where most of the quality lives" and the ability to redo a single line as what makes a tool usable ([DEV Community, fullcastbook](https://dev.to/fullcastbook/turning-a-manuscript-into-a-multi-voice-ai-audiobook-speaker-detection-per-character-voices-and-47f)). The only figure for pauses is one tool's default of 500 ms at a speaker change and 250 ms between lines of the same speaker; a sentence with a short tag in the middle would contain two changes, so pause length should follow punctuation ([Alexandria](https://github.com/Finrandojin/alexandria-audiobook)). No study tests distinct voices for listeners with dyslexia, ADHD or low vision, so no accessibility claim can be made yet.

## Build the fallback path first, the casting second, the acting last

The first version should be the narrowest thing that is never worse than today. It finds quotes in code, after detecting each book's dominant quotation style once, and treats a quote left open at a paragraph end as the same speaker continuing. If a book has no reliable quotation marks, or the chapter's marks do not balance, the feature stays off for that book. It splits text so the character voice gets only the quoted words and the narrator keeps tags and narration, then merges neighbouring pieces in the same voice.

A background pass builds the character list: a stable ID, canonical name, aliases, gender with "unknown" allowed, a four-band age and a line count. Attribution then runs per chapter with numbered quotes, the closed list plus reserved answers for narrator, unknown, group and new character, the previous chunk's answers carried forward, thinking off and structured output on. A code rule handles quotes with a named tag beside them. Only tagged or high-confidence quotes get a character voice; everything else is read by the narrator. Results are stored per chapter under a hash of the chapter text and prompt version, so each book is paid for once.

Casting in the first version uses catalog voices only. Fetch the Inworld catalog once, filter by gender and age group, and let the model pick from a shortlist using the voice descriptions and the character's lines. Give unique voices to the dozen or so characters with the most lines, share a small pool among the rest by scene, and never cast an accent the text does not state. Store voice ID and model on the character record, and never change a voice that has been heard unless the user asks. Speech goes through script requests on `inworld-tts-2` over HTTP streaming with per-utterance output, stable delivery for the narrator, and no per-line steering yet. The reader needs three controls from day one: an off switch, a cast list where any voice can be changed for the whole book, and a way to correct the speaker of one line. Two checks belong before any of this ships: a labelled test of a few real chapters against the chosen language model, and a listening test of script-request joins on a phone.

Later work, in rough order of value: per-line delivery direction taken from tags such as "she whispered", or Inworld's Reasoning mode, once the open questions about steering inside scripts are answered. Then a "subtle" mode that keeps one voice and varies delivery per character, matching partially voiced narration. Then designed voices for leads or for gaps the catalog cannot fill, within the plan's voice limit. Then a per-book pronunciation dictionary so every voice says names the same way, per-voice loudness gain, and series-wide voice memory. A language-model fallback for books without quotation marks and for other languages should wait for its own test set, since nothing is published. A hosted ModernBookNLP service is worth considering only if language-model accuracy on untagged dialogue proves too low, since it needs a GPU service outside the edge-function stack.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the difficulty sits. Attribution accuracy looked like the hard problem, and it is now good enough on tagged dialogue and cheap enough everywhere that it no longer decides feasibility. The hard problems are the ones nobody has published on: how a just-ahead pipeline behaves in real time, how a hosted model performs on modern books with a machine-built character list, and how listeners react to an occasional wrong voice or a line that falls back to the narrator. Each of those is answerable only by building and measuring, which favours a first version small enough to instrument.

The position also has a clock on it. Inworld already sells the offline version of this feature on the same model, and a funded startup shipped a progressive version two weeks before this report. What remains open is the in-reader experience: character voices from any page with no wait, a spoiler-safe cast list, and corrections that cost nothing because only played audio is ever synthesised.
